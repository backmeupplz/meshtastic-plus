Why terrain beats every link budget formula

A LoRa receiver can pull a Long Fast packet out of the noise at -131 dBm, which gives a standard 22 dBm radio over 150 dB of link budget. In empty space at 906.875 MHz, 150 dB is enough to cross hundreds of kilometers. That is how the 331 km ground-to-ground Meshtastic record documented on meshtastic.org happened: two RAK4631 nodes on high peaks with unobstructed line of sight across open air.

Down at street level, three physical limits take over long before you run out of decibels. First is Earth's curvature: two people holding radios at 1.8 m eye height on flat ground only have a radio horizon of about 11 km before the ground itself sits between them. Second is the first Fresnel zone, the football-shaped volume around the direct line between antennas; at 906.875 MHz over a 3 km path, its radius at the midpoint is 15.8 m, and blocking more than roughly 40 percent of that zone costs extra signal even when you can visually see the far hill. Third is clutter: brick walls, steel roofs, dense hills and wet foliage absorb 868 and 915 MHz signals fast, which is why the same radio that covers 40 km from a lookout tower can struggle across 800 meters of downtown apartment blocks.

How the numbers are calculated, and what is approximate

The exact RF part comes straight from the link budget equation. Because Meshtastic's published preset link budgets assume 22 dBm transmit power and 0 dBi antennas, the calculator derives each preset's receiver sensitivity as RX_sens = 22 - LinkBudget_ref, then computes your setup's link budget as TX_dBm + TX_gain + RX_gain - Cable_loss - RX_sens. Free-space path loss at distance d (km) and frequency f (MHz) is 32.44 + 20 * log10(d) + 20 * log10(f), and the midpoint radius of the first Fresnel zone in meters is 17.32 * sqrt(d / (4 * f_GHz)).

The terrain range band is explicitly an approximation. For clear line of sight, the calculator solves the free-space equation with 20 dB (conservative) and 10 dB (optimistic) of fade margin, which tells you what the radio can reach only if both nodes sit high enough to clear the horizon and Fresnel zone. For rural, suburban and dense urban environments, it uses a standard log-distance path-loss model referenced to free-space loss at 1 meter (L0) with a 10 dB fade margin and path-loss exponent bands: n = 2.8 to 3.1 for rural terrain with scattered trees, n = 3.2 to 3.6 for suburbs, and n = 3.7 to 4.2 for dense urban buildings. It does not model your specific elevation profile, local RF noise floor, antenna SWR mismatch or multipath fading, so treat any non-line-of-sight range band as a rough planning bracket and check elevation profiles before mounting a permanent rooftop or ridge node.

Worked examples

Running four common setups through the calculator shows how much more environment changes distance than a few decibels of radio power:

  • Two suburban handhelds on Long Fast (US 906.875 MHz): 22 dBm, 2 dBi antennas on both ends and 1 dB combined loss give a 156.0 dB link budget (-131.0 dBm sensitivity). At 3 km, free-space path loss is 101.1 dB (+54.9 dB free-space margin), the Fresnel radius is 15.8 m, and suburban clutter narrows the expected range band to 1.5–3.8 km (-0.8 to +13.1 dB margin at 3 km).
  • Busy city mesh on Medium Fast (US 906.875 MHz): switching from Long Fast to Medium Fast trades 5 dB of link budget (151.0 dB total, -126.0 dBm sensitivity) for more than three times the data rate (3.52 kbps vs 1.07 kbps). In dense urban buildings, the estimated band is 0.4–0.9 km.
  • Rural Europe on Long Moderate (EU 869.525 MHz): 22 dBm with 3 dBi antennas and 1.5 dB loss produces a 160.5 dB link budget (-134.0 dBm sensitivity), giving a 7.0–18.2 km rural band and +8.3 to +20.0 dB margin at 8 km.
  • Hilltop relay to a handheld (Long Fast, 906.875 MHz, clear LOS): 22 dBm with a 5.8 dBi mast antenna on the hill, a 2 dBi whip on the handheld and 2 dB coax loss yields a 158.8 dB budget. At 40 km with clear sight lines, free-space path loss is 123.6 dB, leaving +35.2 dB of margin as long as the path clears the 57.5 m midpoint Fresnel radius.

Questions

How far does a Meshtastic radio reach in real life?

On Long Fast at 22 dBm with stock 2 dBi antennas, most people see roughly 0.4 to 1.2 km in dense cities, 1.5 to 3.8 km across suburbs, 4 to 10 km in open rural areas, and tens to hundreds of kilometers from high ridges with unobstructed line of sight.

Does doubling transmit power double Meshtastic range?

No. Doubling transmit power adds 3 dB to your link budget, which increases free-space distance by about 41 percent and cluttered suburban distance by around 20 to 25 percent. Putting your antenna higher up with a clear view almost always beats turning up power.

Which Meshtastic modem preset gives the best range?

Among the nine general-purpose presets in Meshtastic 2.8, Long Moderate has the highest published link budget at 156 dB (3 dB above Long Fast), at the cost of slower 0.34 kbps airtime. In busy metro meshes, Medium Fast (148 dB, 3.52 kbps) often gets more messages through because packets occupy the channel for less time.

Why does a higher-gain antenna sometimes perform worse indoors or in hills?

Higher-gain omnidirectional antennas focus energy into a flatter pancake toward the horizon. If you are hiking up a steep ridge below a relay, or carrying the radio at an angle in a pack, a narrow beam can miss the other node where a 2 to 3 dBi antenna still connects.
